On this planet, we have never been good at staying still.

We mapped every ocean. We crossed every desert. And when there was nowhere left to walk, we looked up.

In 1969, two human beings stepped onto the Moon. They stayed for twenty-one hours. They planted a flag, collected rocks, set up experiments, and took photographs of their own footprints.

Then they climbed back into a tiny capsule and flew home.

But they didn't take everything with them.

The bottom half of the Eagle — the golden descent stage that carried them down — was never meant to come home. It is still standing at Tranquility Base, exactly where it landed.

Beside it, they placed the first seismometer on another world. When the Apollo 12 crew deliberately crashed their ascent stage into the surface seventy-three kilometres away, the Moon rang like a bell for fifty-five minutes. That long ringing told us the crust is dry and deeply fractured.

At Hadley Rille, Apollo 15 parked a car on the Moon in 1971. It has not moved an inch in over fifty years.

On Mars, the story is different. Mars has weather. For fourteen years, a rover called Opportunity drove across the red planet, far outliving its ninety-day mission. Then in 2018, a planet-wide dust storm blotted out the sun. On June the tenth, Opportunity sent one final message home. We sent over a thousand commands trying to wake it. It never answered.

And further out — much further — Voyager 1 launched in 1977 and just kept going. This week it reaches one full light-day from Earth: twenty-six billion kilometres away. Two of its ten instruments are still transmitting. It has never stopped.

But back on the Moon, there is one piece of Apollo hardware that still works — not because it has power or electronics, but because it doesn't need any.

It is a panel of mirrors. Corner cubes, angled so that any light that hits them bounces straight back the way it came.

Several times a month, scientists fire a laser from Earth at that panel. The beam crosses three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometres of empty space. Hits the mirrors. And exactly two and a half seconds later, the light comes back.

From those return pulses, we know the Moon is drifting away from us at three point eight centimetres a year. We have been measuring it for over fifty years. And the mirrors have never failed, because there is nothing in them to fail.

One thing still answers.

They are abandoned, but not forgotten.
