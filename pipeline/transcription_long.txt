On this planet, we have never been good at staying still.
We sailed across every ocean, we walked across every desert.
And when there was nowhere left on Earth to explore,
we looked up at the stars.
In 1969, two human beings landed on the moon.
They stayed for 21 hours.
They planted a flag, collected bags of moon rocks,
and took pictures of their own footprints in the dust.
Then they flew home,
but they couldn't take everything with them.
The bottom half of the eagle,
the golden descent stage that carried them down
to the moon was never meant to come home.
When it was time to leave,
only the top cabin blasted into space.
The bottom half, with its golden legs and ladder,
stayed behind.
It is still standing at Tranquility Base today,
exactly where they left it.
Beside the lander, the astronauts placed a seismometer.
That is an electronic ear pressed against the ground
to listen for moon quakes.
Later, when NASA crashed a rocket stage into the moon
about 70 kilometers away,
roughly an hour's drive in a car,
the dry lunar rock had no water to absorb the shake.
The entire moon rang like a giant playground bell
for nearly an hour.
That long ringing told us the crust is dry
and deeply fractured.
Further away at a moon valley lunar crater named Hadley Reel,
Apollo 15 parked a car on the moon in 1971.
It was an electric space buggy with wire mesh tires.
It has not moved an inch in over 50 years.
If you visited today,
it would look like the astronauts
just stepped away for a moment.
On Mars, the story is different.
Mars has weather.
For 14 years, a little robot rover named Opportunity
rolled across the red dust.
It was only built to last 90 days
about the length of a summer vacation.
Instead, it explored for over 5,000 days.
But in 2018, a dust storm so huge
it covered the entire planet blocked out the sun.
Opportunity solar panels went dark.
And on June 10th, it sent one final message home
before falling asleep forever.
We sent over 1,000 commands trying to wake it.
It never answered.
And further out, passed all the planets.
A robotic probe named Voyager 1 launched in 1977
and just kept flying.
It is now 26 billion kilometers away.
That is so far that if you shown the fastest beam
of light in the universe,
it would take 24 whole hours.
One full day and night just to reach it.
Yet even from that unimaginable distance,
its tiny radio is still whispering science back to us.
Two of its 10 instruments are still transmitting.
Back on the moon,
there is one piece of Apollo equipment
that still works perfectly.
Not because it has nuclear batteries or supercomputers,
but because it doesn't need any power at all.
It is a tray of special glass prisms
called corner cube reflectors,
just like the safety reflectors on the wheels
of your bicycle that shine when headlights hit them.
Several times a month,
scientists shoot a beam of green laser light
straight at that little tray.
The beam travels across almost 400,000 kilometers of space,
far enough to wrap around the Earth 10 times.
It hits the mirrors.
And exactly two and a half seconds later,
the light bounces straight back into our telescopes on Earth.
Because of those bouncing laser beams,
we know the moon is slowly drifting away from us
by about four centimeters every year,
about as fast as your fingernails grow.
